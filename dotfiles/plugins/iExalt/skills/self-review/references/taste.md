# Review taste

The user's defaults. Their edits during a review override these; if an edit contradicts a rule here
and is likely to recur, suggest updating this file.

## Shape

- Open the contributions answer with one sentence on the period: what the user worked on, with
  exact dates for each stretch, and the thread that ties it together.
- Each contribution starts with a **bold lead sentence that states the outcome**, in the first
  person, as something a reader outside the team would care about ("Three teams now deploy through
  the pipeline I built", not "Worked on the deploy pipeline"). One or two sentences of
  context follow.
- Under the lead, bullets each start with a **bold label that is a short "I + verb" claim** ("I cut
  the build time."), then the evidence: what, how much, when.
- Order contributions by impact on people outside the team, then by size.
- End the contributions answer with strengths, each proved by an example from above, and a short
  "Colleagues noticed" item with quoted praise.

## Claims

- Lead with outcomes and numbers; put the method after.
- Every claim has a link in the appendix. A claim with no link is cut or asked about.
- Never claim more than the evidence: say "one of the leads" when there were several, "helped" when
  the user contributed but did not own it, and the measured scope ("two of the five services"),
  not an extrapolation.
- Prefer the user's own measurement over a mined figure, and keep its precision.
- Use exact dates and periods. Write "February to June", not "early in the year".
- Quote praise verbatim with who said it, their role if it helps, and the occasion. If a quote was
  about a project, frame it as about the project.
- Name commendations that went up the chain, and who forwarded them to whom.

## Challenges

- Give a real frustration its own heading, with what was invested, how long, at what level, and how
  it ended. Do not bury it in a list.
- Leave out generic challenges (priorities changed, busy quarter) unless the user insists.
- Add a **What I'd do differently** line only when the user has an honest one; it should be a
  concrete change of behavior, not a promise to communicate better.
- Name invisible work as a challenge when it applies: plumbing that is hard to demo or present.

## Goals

- Number the goals and lead each with a bold imperative. Put the user's first priority first.
- Tie each goal to a past example that shows the user can do it.
- End with **What I need from my manager**: specific asks, such as scope, environments or
  visibility.

## Prose

- Punchy and outcome-first, not a dry list of facts, but no inflation: no "pivotal", "spearheaded",
  "passionate", "leveraged".
- Avoid repeating the same word or opener in adjacent bullets.
- Run prose-fix in embedded mode before every export.
- Plain-text export: no markdown, URLs, em or en dashes; bullets become "•".

## Peer reviews

- Write from the user's vantage point: "I saw", "my team relied on", not a rating of work the user
  never saw.
- Lead with the two or three things the colleague did that made the biggest difference, each with a
  concrete moment, date and outcome.
- Quote others' praise only from channels the user could see, and attribute it.
- Make the growth item specific and actionable, with what would change if they did it. One is
  usually enough.
- Keep it shorter than a self-review: their manager reads many. Set a word target when planning
  (the form's limit, or the length the user asked for), check the export against it before
  handoff, and trim to it unless the extra detail serves a need the user stated.
