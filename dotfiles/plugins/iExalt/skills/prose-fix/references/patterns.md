# Pattern catalog

Each pattern is one form of a model's default choice: the phrasing that fits the widest range
of readers. Read the passage before classifying it. A watched phrase inside a quotation, title,
proper name, or discussion of the phrase is not a tell, and neither is text written before
late 2022.

Patterns in sections A to C are structural and justify an edit on one sighting. A pattern
marked *weak alone* needs other tells nearby before you act. Treat word lists as search aids,
not bans; do not swap one watched word for its synonym.

Two tests help with any pattern:

- **Flatten test.** State the claim without the cadence. If nothing specific remains, the form
  was standing in for missing reasoning.
- **Relation test.** Restate the implied link with `because`, `although`, `when`, `if`, or `so`.
  If that requires inventing a relation, the original only gestured at one.

## A. Staging instead of stating

### A1. Not X but Y

**Watch:** not X but Y; not just/only/merely X but Y; it's not X, it's Y; X rather than Y; the
same contrast split across sentences ("This doesn't mean X. It means Y."); a negative list
before the reveal ("Not a X. Not a Y. A Z."); a clipped negative tail (", no guessing").
**Fix:** State Y. Keep the contrast only when X is a belief the reader actually holds or a
mistake they would actually make, or when both halves carry information. In agent instructions
most contrasts are rules ("null, not zero"); keep those.

> Before: Caching isn't just a performance trick. It's a reliability strategy.
> After: The cache keeps pages loading when the database is down.

### A2. Closers, fragments, and quotables

**Watch:** a one-line paragraph that restates the last one; "That's the real win."; "Let that
sink in."; "Full stop."; a sentence after an example that names what it showed ("This shows
the importance of..."); a row of fragments ("No config. No setup. Just results."); every
paragraph ending on a line built to be quoted.
**Fix:** Cut a closer that repeats. Keep a short line that adds a fact or consequence. Merge
fragments into a sentence with a specific claim.

### A3. Sayings that sound deep

**Watch:** the real question is, at its core, what really matters, the heart of the matter, the
uncomfortable truth, X is the Y of Z ("trust is the currency of teams"), X becomes a trap.
**Fix:** Replace the saying with the specific claim it gestures at.

### A4. Throat-clearing and staged openers

**Watch:** let's dive in, let's break this down, here's what you need to know, here's the thing,
here's why, it turns out, the truth is, let me be clear, honestly?, look, real talk, think
about it, what if I told you, a question answered in the next sentence.
**Fix:** Delete the run-up and start with the point. "Honestly" inside an ordinary casual
sentence is fine; the tell is the standalone opener.

### A5. Arguing with no one

**Watch:** to be clear, I'm not saying, don't get me wrong, this isn't about, some might say,
a tempting approach would be, you might think.
**Fix:** Remove an objection or alternative the text never raised. Keep one a reader would
actually weigh, or one the text attributes and answers.

### A6. Telling instead of showing

**Watch:** vague declaratives ("The implications are significant.", "The stakes are high.",
"This is genuinely hard."); "this is what X actually looks like".
**Fix:** Name the implication, stake, or difficulty, or cut the sentence.

## B. Inflation and borrowed authority

The fact underneath is usually sound. Keep it and remove the dressing.

### B1. Inflated significance

**Watch:** pivotal or crucial moment, plays a key role, testament to, marks a shift, reflects a
broader trend, evolving landscape, lasting legacy, setting the stage; stock "challenges and
outlook" sections; send-offs ("The future looks bright.").
**Fix:** State the mechanism or result and let the reader judge its importance. End on the last
concrete fact or next step. An evaluative word stays when the same passage supplies the
evidence ("robust" next to the retry logic it describes).

### B2. Concession then reassurance

**Watch:** "Despite these challenges, X continues to thrive"; a difficulty immediately
neutralized by generic optimism; "And that's okay."
**Fix:** Let the limitation stand, or support the reassurance with evidence.

### B3. Interpretive -ing tails

**Watch:** highlighting, underscoring, emphasizing, showcasing, reflecting, ensuring,
fostering, symbolizing, contributing to.
**Fix:** Remove the tail, or state the interpretation as its own supported sentence.

### B4. Vague attribution and connection

**Watch:** experts argue, studies show, observers note, industry reports; associated with,
linked to, tied to; lists of outlets someone was "featured in"; follower counts.
**Fix:** Name the supplied source and what it said, or cut the claim. Name the relationship
("founded", "chairs") only if the source gives it; otherwise keep the vague wording rather
than invent a role.

### B5. False agency

**Watch:** inanimate things doing human verbs: "the data tells us", "the decision emerged",
"the complaint became a fix", "the culture shifted", "the market rewards".
**Fix:** Name who acted. Passive or abstract subjects stay when the actor is unknown,
irrelevant, or deliberately protected.

### B6. False range

**Watch:** "from X to Y" spanning items that are not on one scale ("from startups to
governments, from art to logistics").
**Fix:** Name the items directly, or keep the one that matters.

### B7. Sales language and jargon

**Watch:** nestled, in the heart of, vibrant, rich (figurative), breathtaking, stunning,
renowned, must-visit, groundbreaking, game-changer, seamless, unlock, commitment to; navigate
(challenges), unpack, lean into, double down, deep dive, circle back, moving forward.
**Fix:** Say what the thing is or does, in plain words.

### B8. Stock AI vocabulary

**Watch:** additionally, align with, bolster, crucial, delve, enhance, garner, highlight (verb),
interplay, intricate, key (adjective), landscape (abstract), meticulous, pivotal, realm,
showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore (verb), valuable, vibrant; figurative robust or
gated. *Weak alone*; clusters are the signal.
**Fix:** Rewrite the claim. A formal word outside these lists is not a tell.

### B9. Avoiding is and has

**Watch:** serves as, stands as, functions as, represents a; boasts, features, offers.
**Fix:** Use *is*, *are*, and *has*.

## C. Rhythm by rule

### C1. Forced triads

**Watch:** lists that keep landing on exactly three polished items; three parallel examples;
three short facts followed by a lesson.
**Fix:** Keep the items that add a distinct idea. When trimming, two often beats three. Keep a
real list of three, and keep every item of a complete list in instructions or reference text.

### C2. Dash and colon pivots

**Rule:** No em dashes (—) or en dashes (–) in prose, including spaced dashes and ` -- `, unless
the writer's sample or house style uses them; then match their rate. Use a period, comma,
colon, or parentheses, or rewrite. Number ranges become "2 to 4". Leave dashes in code,
commands, paths, and URLs alone.
**Also watch:** a statement followed by a punchy colon reveal, used repeatedly.

### C3. Metronomic rhythm

**Watch:** consecutive sentences of matching length and syntax; sections with identical length
and internal order; paragraphs that could trade places without changing the argument; several
sentences opening with the same subject; staccato runs of short sentences.
**Fix:** Merge, reorder, or name the relation at the weak join. Vary length because the thought
varies, never to look irregular. Writers repeat an opening on purpose sometimes; keep that.

### C4. Stacked qualifiers and filler

**Watch:** could potentially, might arguably, it's possible that; in order to, due to the fact
that, at this point in time, has the ability to, it's worth noting that, it is important to
note, when it comes to, at the end of the day.
**Fix:** Keep one qualifier the source supports; cut the rest. Use the short form ("to",
"because", "now", "can").

### C5. Empty adverbs

**Watch:** really, just, literally, genuinely, honestly, simply, actually, truly, deeply,
fundamentally, inherently, interestingly, importantly, crucially.
**Fix:** Cut adverbs that only add emphasis or sincerity. Keep adverbs that set a condition or
scope ("only", "explicitly", "silently", "never").

### C6. Lazy extremes

**Watch:** every, always, never, everyone, nobody, used as sweeping claims in descriptive prose.
**Fix:** Use the real scope ("most", "in our tests"). In rules and instructions these words are
the rule; keep them.

### C7. Synonym cycling

**Watch:** one thing renamed in adjacent sentences ("the tool", "the utility", "the
solution").
**Fix:** Pick the clearest term and repeat it.

### C8. Passive voice and missing subjects

**Watch:** "No configuration needed."; "Mistakes were made."; "It is believed that".
**Fix:** Name the actor when it makes the sentence clearer. *Weak alone.*

### C9. Hyphenated pairs after the noun

**Watch:** "the report is high-quality", "the process is well-documented".
**Fix:** Drop the hyphen after the noun; keep it before ("a high-quality report"). *Weak alone.*

## D. Formatting by rule

### D1. Bold as decoration

**Watch:** bolded phrases with no reason; bullet lists where every item has a bold label and a
colon.
**Fix:** Remove the bold. Turn a labeled list into prose when the labels carry nothing of their
own. Keep labels that name distinct terms or that other text refers to.

### D2. Decorative headings and markup

**Watch:** title case against house style; emoji or arrows as decoration; a rule between every
section; skipped heading levels; tables that a sentence would do; headings written for effect
("The decision, on one screen").
**Fix:** Use sentence case unless the house style differs, name what the section holds, and
remove decoration. Do not rename a heading other text refers to.

### D3. Heading restated

**Watch:** a heading followed by a one-line paragraph that repeats it ("## Performance /
Speed matters.").
**Fix:** Cut the restatement.

### D4. Quotes

**Watch:** curly quotes where the target uses straight quotes, or the reverse. *Weak alone.*
**Fix:** Match the platform: straight in code-oriented and wiki contexts.

## E. Leftovers from chat and drafting

Remove these outright.

### E1. Chatbot residue

**Watch:** Certainly!, Great question!, You're absolutely right, Here is a..., I hope this
helps, Let me know if, Would you like me to..., Should I continue?
**Fix:** Remove the wrapper and keep the content.

### E2. Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses

**Watch:** as of my last update, based on available information, not widely documented,
it appears that, likely grew up.
**Fix:** State what the source does not show, or cut the sentence. Never fill the gap with a
plausible guess.

### E3. Writing about the document

**Watch:** "In this section, we'll...", "As we'll see", "The rest of this post explains",
"Let me walk you through"; what the text replaced ("was added to replace"); how it was
assembled ("compiled from"); a layout the reader can see ("the table below compares").
**Fix:** Delete it and let the text move. Keep a change history only in changelogs, release
notes, and migration guides, and keep a caveat that changes what the reader should do.

### E4. Markup artifacts

**Watch:** internal citation markers, tool output labels, unused reference links, tracking
parameters such as `utm_source` in URLs.
**Fix:** Remove them.

## F. Writing for the wrong reader

### F1. Re-explaining what the reader knows

**Watch:** a reply that restates the problem, walks through the diagnosis, and lays out
evidence before the decision; background the other person wrote; the answer in the last line.
**Fix:** Lead with the decision. Keep only the reasoning that would change whether the reader
agrees and any link they need to act. Act on this only when you can see the conversation or
the text is plainly a reply.

### F2. Narrator from a distance

**Watch:** "Nobody designed this.", "People tend to...", lecturer voice in personal writing.
**Fix:** Put the reader in the scene with "you" or a specific person, when the register allows.

## Not signals

Correct grammar, formal language, transition words on their own, restrained prose, a single
dash, and a missing citation are not evidence of AI writing. Protect what carries a writer's
voice: an unusual checkable detail, a real aside or self-correction, mixed feelings, deliberate
repetition of the right word, trade language, dated references, and an earned joke, contrast,
or closing line.
